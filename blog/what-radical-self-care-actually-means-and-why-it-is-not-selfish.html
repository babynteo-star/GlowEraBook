What Radical Self-Care Actually Means (And Why It Is Not Selfish)

In This Article

  1. The word "selfish" is doing someone else's work
  2. Radical doesn't mean extreme, it means root
  3. Where the guilt was installed
  4. What it actually looks like in a life

A woman I'll call Marisol once told me that the first time she said no to her mother without an explanation attached, she had to sit down afterward. Not because anything bad happened. Her mother didn't yell, didn't even push back much. Marisol sat down because her whole body was shaking, the way it shakes after you've done something dangerous, even though all she'd done was decline an invitation. That's the moment I think of when people ask me what radical self-care actually means. It rarely looks like what we picture. It looks like a woman sitting at her kitchen table, hands trembling, because she just told the truth for the first time in years.

We've been sold an image of self-care that is soft around the edges and easy to sell back to us: candles, bath salts, a Sunday spent doing a face mask while a rom-com plays in the background. There is nothing wrong with any of that. But it is not, on its own, self-care in the sense that actually changes a life. It's more like self-maintenance, a way of resetting your nervous system so you can go back into the same unsustainable patterns on Monday. Radical self-care is a different animal entirely. It is not about adding a pleasant ritual on top of your life. It is about questioning the structure of the life itself, and having the nerve to change what needs changing.

The word "selfish" is doing someone else's work

Here's something worth sitting with: the accusation of selfishness almost never comes from a place of clear-eyed observation. It comes from someone who has grown used to your availability and is unsettled by its absence. When you start setting limits, the people who benefited most from you having none will often be the first to reach for that word. Not because you've actually become self-centered, but because your new behavior requires something uncomfortable of them: adjusting.

This doesn't mean every person who feels hurt by your boundary is manipulating you. Sometimes the hurt is real and reasonable, and you'll need to sit with that too, gently, without letting it undo the boundary. But it does mean you should get suspicious of the word "selfish" itself. Ask what it's protecting. Usually, it's protecting someone else's comfort, at the cost of yours.

The accusation of selfishness is rarely about who you are. It's about what someone else is used to.

Radical doesn't mean extreme, it means root

People hear "radical self-care" and picture something dramatic: quitting the job, moving to another country, cutting off half the family in one furious announcement. Sometimes radical self-care does involve a big, visible change. But the word radical comes from the Latin radix, meaning root. Radical self-care is care that goes to the root of things, rather than treating symptoms on the surface.

Surface-level self-care asks, "How do I feel better after a hard day?" Root-level self-care asks a harder question: "Why is every day hard in this particular way, and what would need to be true for that to change?" The first question gets you a bath and an early bedtime. The second gets you into a conversation with your partner about the division of labor in your house, or a serious look at whether the job that exhausts you is actually worth what it's costing your body. It is slower work. It is not always comfortable. It is, in the truest sense, care.

Where the guilt was installed

Almost nobody arrives at adulthood with a natural, uncomplicated relationship to their own needs. Most of us were taught, in small moments that added up over years, that our worth was tied to our usefulness. The child who was praised for being "so easy," "so mature," "no trouble at all." The oldest daughter who became a second parent before she'd finished being a child herself. The employee who was told, implicitly or explicitly, that ambition and self-sacrifice were the same thing.

If you were raised this way, choosing yourself doesn't feel neutral. It feels like a small betrayal of everyone who came to rely on the version of you that never asked for anything. That guilt is not a signal that you're doing something wrong. It's a signal that you're doing something your nervous system has never practiced before. Guilt, in this context, is often just unfamiliarity wearing a moral costume.

This is also why self-care that only addresses the surface tends to collapse the moment life gets busy again, which is a pattern I go into more in why your self-care routine keeps failing. If the guilt underneath was never examined, no amount of scheduled rest will hold against it for long.

What it actually looks like in a life

Radical self-care rarely announces itself. It looks like leaving a family dinner an hour earlier than you normally would, without a medical excuse prepared in advance. It looks like telling a friend, "I don't have the capacity to talk about this tonight, can we make a plan for the weekend instead," and then not immediately apologizing for the sentence. It looks like turning down a promotion that would have paid more and cost you every evening with your kids, and not explaining that decision to anyone who didn't ask.

It also looks like something much quieter: noticing, in the middle of an ordinary Tuesday, that you are exhausted, and letting that be enough of a reason to lie down for twenty minutes instead of finding a way to justify it first. Most of us have been trained to require a reason for rest that would satisfy a courtroom. Radical self-care lets tired be the whole sentence.

None of this happens all at once. It's built the way any real practice is built, in small, repeatable choices rather than one dramatic overhaul, which is exactly the territory I walk through in how to build a self-care practice that survives your busiest weeks. The goal was never perfection. It was consistency with yourself, especially on the days you're tempted to abandon it first.

Reflection Prompts

  • Think of the last time someone called you (or you called yourself) selfish. What need were you actually meeting in that moment?
  • Whose voice do you hear when you imagine disappointing someone? Where did you first learn that disappointing people was dangerous?
  • If your self-care only ever happens after you've collapsed from exhaustion, what would it look like to offer it to yourself before that point?
  • What is one root-level question about your life you've been avoiding because the surface-level fixes are easier?

Try This Today

Pick one small, specific moment today where you would normally default to "yes" out of guilt rather than genuine willingness. It doesn't have to be dramatic; it can be as small as agreeing to a phone call you don't have the energy for. Instead, pause and say, honestly, "Let me think about that and get back to you," even if you already know your answer is no. Notice what happens in your body while you wait to respond. That gap, between the automatic yes and the considered answer, is where radical self-care actually begins.

I wrote about this exact gap in my book, Glow Era, because I don't think most of us lack the desire to care for ourselves. I think we lack permission, and permission is something you can practice giving yourself in tiny, deliberate doses until it starts to feel like the truth. If you want a fuller roadmap for that work, Glow Era walks through it chapter by chapter, but the roadmap only works if you're willing to sit at your own kitchen table, hands shaking a little, and stay there anyway.

Marisol told me later that nothing bad came of that first no. Her mother called the next week like nothing had happened. But something in Marisol had shifted permanently, the way a lock clicks open and simply cannot click shut the same way again. That's what radical self-care gives you. Not a better mood. A different relationship with your own life.
