Creating a Self-Care Ritual for the Season of Life You Are Actually In

In This Article

  1. Why the old ritual stops working
  2. Naming the season honestly
  3. Building the ritual around the real constraint
  4. Letting the ritual change again, and again

A friend of mine, a new mother I'll call Bea, spent the first six months of her daughter's life quietly furious with herself for not keeping up with the yoga practice she used to swear by. Every time she saw her rolled-up mat in the corner, gathering dust, she felt a small stab of failure, as though the woman who used to do ninety minutes of vinyasa five mornings a week was the real her, and the exhausted, milk-stained woman on the couch at 6 a.m. was some lesser, temporary substitute who just needed to try harder. What she didn't yet understand was that the ritual hadn't failed. The season had changed, and the ritual simply hadn't changed with it.

This happens to almost everyone eventually, and it happens more than once across a life. The self-care that fit you in your twenties, when your body could handle a five a.m. gym session and a late night out on no sleep, rarely fits the same way once you're caring for young kids, or an aging parent, or recovering from an illness, or building a business that eats every spare hour. We tend to treat this mismatch as personal failure. It's actually just information. A ritual that doesn't fit your current season isn't a ritual you failed at. It's a ritual you outgrew, or that outgrew you.

Why the old ritual stops working

Every self-care ritual is really a set of assumptions about your resources: how much time you have, how much energy, how much privacy, how much money, how predictable your days are. When any of those change significantly, and life has a way of changing several at once, the ritual built on the old assumptions stops being realistic, even though it might still be technically possible in theory.

Bea's yoga practice assumed ninety uninterrupted minutes and a body that had slept through the night. Neither of those things existed in her new season. The problem was never that she'd become lazy or undisciplined. The problem was that she was still measuring herself against a ritual designed for a completely different set of circumstances, and grading her current life a failure against a bar that no longer applied to it.

Naming the season honestly

Before you can build a ritual that fits, you have to get honest about what season you're actually in, without romanticizing it or minimizing it. This is harder than it sounds, because a lot of us resist naming a season as demanding, out of a fear that naming it makes us seem like we're complaining, or admitting we can't handle what we signed up for.

Ask yourself plainly: what does this season actually offer me, in terms of time, privacy, energy, and predictability? A season of early parenthood might offer five unpredictable minutes at a time, rather than one long stretch. A season of caregiving for a parent might offer more physical time but almost no emotional bandwidth. A season of new, demanding work might offer money and structure but almost no spontaneity. None of these seasons are worse than the others. They simply have different shapes, and your ritual needs to be cut to fit the actual shape you're in, not the one you remember or the one you hope is coming next.

A ritual built for a season you're no longer in isn't self-care. It's a standard you're failing on purpose.

Building the ritual around the real constraint

Once you know the shape of your season, build the ritual to fit inside it rather than fighting it. If your season only offers unpredictable five-minute windows, your ritual needs to be something that works in five unpredictable minutes: three deep breaths with your hand on your chest while the baby naps, one paragraph read standing at the kitchen counter, a single voice memo to yourself instead of a full journal entry. If your season offers structure but no emotional bandwidth, your ritual might be almost entirely physical: a specific stretch, a short walk, a shower you actually take your time in rather than one more task to rush through.

Bea eventually built a version of care that had nothing to do with her old mat. It was three minutes, most mornings, of sitting with her coffee before anyone else woke up, doing absolutely nothing else. Some days it happened. Some days it didn't. Either way, she stopped comparing it to ninety minutes of vinyasa, because she finally understood they weren't the same category of thing. One was a ritual for a season that no longer existed. The other was a ritual for the actual life in front of her.

Letting the ritual change again, and again

The hardest part of this practice isn't building the right ritual for your current season. It's accepting that you'll have to do this again, probably more than once, because seasons don't hold still. The ritual that fits perfectly this year might need to change again in eighteen months, when the baby becomes a toddler, or the caregiving ends, or the business finally stabilizes. This isn't a sign of instability. It's just the nature of a life that keeps moving.

Building this kind of flexibility into your expectations from the start makes the eventual change much less painful. Instead of grieving a ritual that "stopped working," you can simply notice, calmly, "this season is shifting, so my care needs to shift with it," and go looking for the next version. This is closely tied to how to say yes to yourself, because choosing a ritual honestly suited to your real season, rather than an idealized one, is itself an act of saying yes to the life you're actually living instead of the one you think you should be living.

Reflection Prompts

  • What season of life are you actually in right now, described honestly, without minimizing or exaggerating it?
  • What self-care ritual from a previous season have you been quietly holding yourself to, even though it no longer fits?
  • What resources does this current season genuinely offer you, in terms of time, privacy, and energy?
  • What would a ritual look like if it were built entirely around this season's real constraints, rather than around guilt for not doing more?

Try This Today

Take five minutes and write down, plainly, the shape of your current season: how much uninterrupted time you realistically have, how predictable your days are, what your energy tends to look like by evening. Then design one ritual, just one, small enough to actually fit inside that shape. If mornings offer you nothing, try an evening version instead, even something as simple as a warm drink you actually sit down for rather than drink standing at the sink. If you want a gentler place to start, a slow self-care day at home style routine, scaled down to fit a single hour instead of a full day, is a good template to steal from.

I wrote a fair amount of Glow Era while I was myself in a season that didn't match any ritual I'd ever built before, which is part of why the book keeps returning to this idea: care that isn't honest about your actual life isn't really care, it's aspiration wearing care's clothes. If this season has you feeling like you're failing at something that was never designed for you in the first place, the book might be a useful companion for figuring out what actually fits now.

Bea's mat is still in the corner. She hasn't thrown it out, and she doesn't need to. It's just waiting for a season that might suit it again someday, or it might not, and either way, that's fine. The ritual was never the point. The care underneath it was, and that can wear almost any shape a season asks of it.
