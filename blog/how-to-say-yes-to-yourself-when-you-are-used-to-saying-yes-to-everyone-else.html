How to Say Yes to Yourself When You Are Used to Saying Yes to Everyone Else

In This Article

  1. Why yes became a reflex instead of a choice
  2. The physical experience of a real yes
  3. Practicing yes toward yourself
  4. When the old pattern gets reinforced by numbing

A woman named Odalys once described her weeks to me like a series of small transactions she never remembered agreeing to. Yes to covering a coworker's shift. Yes to hosting Thanksgiving again, even though it was her sister's turn by any fair count. Yes to a friend's late-night phone call when she had an early meeting the next day. By Sunday night, she'd look back and realize she couldn't name a single thing she'd done that week purely because she wanted to. Every yes had gone somewhere else. Not one had come back to her.

This is such a common pattern that it can start to feel like a personality trait rather than what it actually is, which is a habit, formed early, reinforced constantly, and entirely capable of being unlearned. If you're used to saying yes to everyone else, saying yes to yourself doesn't feel like relief the first few times you try it. It feels like static, like a frequency your body doesn't recognize yet. That discomfort is not a sign you're doing it wrong. It's a sign you're doing something genuinely new.

Why yes became a reflex instead of a choice

For a lot of people, yes stopped being a real answer somewhere along the way and became something closer to a reflex, the automatic response that keeps the peace, avoids conflict, or maintains an identity as the reliable one, the easy one, the one who never causes trouble. Reflexes don't involve much thought. That's precisely the problem. A yes given as a reflex was never actually a decision, which means the part of you that has real preferences, real limits, real desires, never gets consulted at all.

This usually traces back to an early lesson that love or safety was conditional on agreeableness. Maybe you learned that the version of you who said no got less warmth, more punishment, more distance. Children are extraordinary pattern-recognizers, and if saying no reliably cost you something as a kid, your nervous system will keep flagging it as dangerous well into adulthood, long after the original threat is gone.

The physical experience of a real yes

One of the most useful things you can learn is what an honest yes actually feels like in your body, because most people who struggle with this have never really noticed the difference. A reflexive yes, the kind given out of guilt or fear or habit, tends to feel like a slight tightening, a small internal wince, even while your mouth is already forming the word. A genuine yes tends to feel more like an opening, a slight loosening in the chest, even if the thing you're agreeing to is hard.

This distinction takes practice to notice, because most of us are so used to overriding the physical signal that we've stopped registering it consciously. Odalys started, at my suggestion, pausing for three full seconds before answering any request that wasn't an emergency, just to give her body time to react before her habit did. Three seconds isn't much, but it was enough for her to start noticing the wince, and eventually, enough to start honoring it.

A yes that costs you a small wince every time isn't kindness. It's a debt you keep agreeing to pay.

Practicing yes toward yourself

Saying no to others is only half of this work, and it's often the half that gets all the attention. The other half, quieter and just as important, is practicing yes toward yourself specifically, on purpose, in small enough doses that it doesn't trigger the same alarm system as a big, dramatic change.

This might look like saying yes to the twenty minutes of reading you've been putting off in favor of one more task. Yes to the slightly more expensive option that would actually make your week easier. Yes to leaving a gathering when you're ready to leave, rather than when it would be most convenient for everyone else. None of these are large decisions on their own. Their power is cumulative. Each one is a small piece of evidence, presented to a nervous system that doesn't yet believe it, that you are also allowed to be the beneficiary of your own choices, not just the supplier of everyone else's.

This is the same territory covered in what radical self-care actually means, because saying yes to yourself, in this deeper sense, isn't an indulgence. It's the root behavior that everything else grows out of. You cannot build a sustainable life around a self who never gets consulted.

When the old pattern gets reinforced by numbing

It's worth naming honestly that a lot of people who struggle to say yes to themselves end up compensating in less useful ways, reaching for something that dulls the resentment of a life built entirely around other people's needs, rather than addressing the pattern directly. A glass of wine to take the edge off another day of over-giving. A second glass to make the resentment quieter still. Over time, this becomes its own trap, one I've written about at more length in how alcohol quietly dims your glow, because numbing the resentment doesn't resolve it, it just delays the reckoning and adds a second problem on top of the first one.

The more sustainable path is slower and less immediately soothing: noticing the resentment when it shows up, tracing it back to the yes that caused it, and using that information to make a different choice next time, rather than a substance to make the current choice more bearable.

Reflection Prompts

  • Think of the last three things you agreed to. How many were a genuine, wince-free yes, and how many were a reflex?
  • What did you learn, early in life, about what happened to people who said no?
  • What is one small, low-stakes yes you could offer yourself this week, purely because you want it, with no other justification required?
  • Where in your body do you feel the difference between a real yes and a reflexive one? Can you name it specifically?

Try This Today

The next time someone asks something of you today, whether it's a coworker, a partner, a friend, or your own inner to-do list, pause for three full seconds before answering. In that pause, notice what your body does. A wince, a tightening, a small internal flinch, is worth paying attention to, even if you ultimately still say yes. You're not trying to become someone who says no to everything overnight. You're just trying to reintroduce the pause, the moment where a real decision has room to happen instead of a reflex running the show uncontested.

This exact practice, the three-second pause before the automatic yes, is one I come back to often in Glow Era, because I think most of us don't actually need to be convinced that our own needs matter. We need practice noticing them in real time, before they've already been overridden. If this pattern feels familiar, the book spends real time on rebuilding that pause into something durable.

Odalys still says yes to a lot of things. She hosted Thanksgiving again last year, in fact. The difference is that it was an actual yes this time, chosen and weighed, not a reflex she discovered only after the fact. That distinction sounds small from the outside. From the inside, it's the difference between a life that happens to you and one you're actually living.
